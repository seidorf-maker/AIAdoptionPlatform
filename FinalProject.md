## 2. Diary Entries

### Before Using the Product
*Tuesday night, 11:47 PM*

I can't sleep again. The house is quiet except for the hum of the dishwasher and I'm lying here with my eyes wide open, staring at that water stain on the ceiling I keep meaning to ask Mark about, and all I can think about is Priya's voice in that meeting today, casual as anything: "Oh, I just had ChatGPT draft the variance narrative, saved me like two hours." Two hours. She said it the way you'd mention grabbing coffee. And I just sat there nodding like I knew exactly what she meant, my stomach dropping through the floor, thinking — do I even know if I'm allowed to do that? Would I even know how?

I keep replaying that meeting from a few weeks ago, when Doug asked point blank how my team was "leveraging AI" and I gave some mushy non-answer about "exploring opportunities" and I could see it on his face — that flicker of disappointment, the same one I saw when they gave the Finance Manager job to someone else. Not smarter than me. Just faster. Just further along some road I don't even know how to find the entrance to.

I have a whole book on my nightstand, half-read, spine barely cracked. I've started two LinkedIn Learning courses and abandoned both somewhere around minute six because they start talking about APIs and prompt engineering like I'm supposed to already speak that language. I'm a CPA. I built our entire ERP migration practically single-handedly. I am not stupid. So why does this specific thing make me feel fifteen years old again, raising my hand in a class where everyone already knows the answer?

I keep thinking about Emma and Jake, and how in ten years the world they walk into for work is going to run on this stuff, and their mom couldn't even figure out how to ask a computer a good question without feeling like a fraud.

I don't even know where I'm allowed to start. And I'm too embarrassed to ask.

### During Their First Use of the Product
*Thursday, lunch break, at my desk*

Okay. Deep breath. I actually did it.

IT sent that email about the new AI learning platform rolling out — tied to LinkedIn Learning, they said, with an actual accounting and finance track, real certification at the end — and something about the way it was framed, "here's exactly what to use and how," made my shoulders drop about two inches for the first time in weeks. Sanctioned. Official. Not me sneaking around in the dark hoping I wasn't breaking some rule nobody explained to me.

I logged in during lunch, half expecting the same overwhelming wall of jargon. Instead there was a module that just said "AI for Financial Reporting and Reconciliation" and my throat actually got tight, in a good way, like — oh. This is for me. Someone built this for someone like me.

My hands were still a little shaky typing my first prompt, honestly. I kept second-guessing myself, deleting and retyping, sure I was going to do it "wrong" and some invisible person was going to judge me. Old habits. But the module walked me through it, step by step, no judgment, and I got an actual usable draft of a variance explanation in about four minutes. Four minutes. I stared at the screen a little stunned. I even said "huh" out loud, alone, at my desk, like an idiot.

I'm not going to pretend the anxiety is gone. There's still this voice whispering that I'm behind, that everyone else is further along. But for the first time in months, there was a door. And I walked through it instead of standing outside it, staring at the water stain on my ceiling.

### After Using the Product
*Sunday evening, journal on the porch, tea going cold*

I earned my second certification badge this week. I actually said that out loud to Mark, a little sheepishly, and he looked so proud of me it almost made me tear up over dinner, which — I will absolutely deny that if asked.

In Monday's leadership meeting, Doug asked the AI question again, the same one that flattened me a few weeks ago. This time I didn't fumble. I told them exactly how my team cut our close-cycle reconciliation time down using two of the workflows from the training, and I watched his eyebrows go up, just slightly, the way they do when something actually lands. Priya caught my eye afterward and mouthed "nice" across the table. Priya. The one whose casual "oh I just had ChatGPT do it" used to make my stomach drop.

I keep thinking about that night staring at the ceiling, so sure I was quietly becoming obsolete, so ashamed that I couldn't just figure it out on my own. I wasn't behind because I was incapable. I was behind because nobody had ever handed me a real map, just a vague mandate and a locked door.

I still have moments of doubt — I don't think that voice fully goes away. But it's quieter now. It sounds less like "you don't belong here" and more like "you're still learning, and that's allowed."

I told Emma tonight that Mom got a certificate this week for learning new computer skills at work. She asked what it was for. I said, "Making sure I don't get left behind." She said, "You'd never get left behind, Mom." I don't know if I believed her three months ago. I think I might now.

## 3. Brand Identity

### Executive Summary
Denise's pain isn't technical — it's **permission and belonging**. She doesn't need convincing that AI matters; she needs a sanctioned door instead of a locked one, and proof she walked through it. The brand should feel like a trusted colleague handing her a map, not a tech company selling her a tool. Everything below is built around that single insight: **this is a confidence brand, not an AI brand.**

Just as important to how the brand is positioned in the market: OnRamp is not asking companies to replace what they already pay for. It sits on top of existing LMS infrastructure (LinkedIn Learning, internal LMS) as the role-specific **translator and gap-mapper** — turning a generic course catalog into "here's exactly what to take for your job" and turning scattered completions into one trackable, credentialed picture. That reframes the pitch from "buy a new training tool" to "finally get value out of the one you already have."

### Brand Mission
To give every non-technical employee a sanctioned, judgment-free path to AI confidence — specific to their job, not their job title.

### Brand Vision
A workplace where using AI well is simply part of doing your job competently — no longer a source of quiet shame, but proof of exactly the skill Denise always had: showing up, learning, and doing the work right.

### Brand Personality
Think "the calm, credible colleague," not "the disruptive tech startup."

| Is | Is Not |
|---|---|
| Warm, patient, structured | Flashy, jargon-heavy, cutting-edge-for-its-own-sake |
| Credible (badges, credentials, IT-approved) | Experimental, unofficial, "figure it out yourself" |
| Encouraging, non-judgmental | Condescending, gatekeepy |
| Role-specific, practical | Generic, one-size-fits-all |
| A translator on top of existing tools | A replacement demanding a new system |

**Brand archetype:** The Mentor/Guide (trusted teacher, not visionary genius). Denise doesn't want to be dazzled — she wants to be *walked through it*.

### Voice & Tone
- Plain language over jargon. Never say "leverage generative AI capabilities" — say "let AI draft the first pass so you can review it faster."
- Permission-giving phrasing: "Here's exactly what to use," "This is approved," "Step by step."
- Never condescending about the starting point — no "AI for beginners" framing that implies deficiency. Frame it as *fluency*, not remediation.
- Quietly celebratory at milestones — small, real acknowledgment (a badge, a certificate, a "nice work") rather than gamified hype.

### Positioning Statement
For non-technical professionals at mid-to-large companies who feel pressure to "use AI more" with no clear, sanctioned way to start, **OnRamp** is the role-based AI adoption platform that sits on top of the training tools companies already own (LinkedIn Learning, internal LMS), maps each employee's role-specific AI skill gaps against what they've already completed, and turns the result into practical, job-specific skill and real certification — unlike generic AI courses or vague corporate mandates, which leave people anxious, unguided, and afraid to ask, and unlike a brand-new LMS, which asks companies to abandon what they already pay for.

### Messaging Pillars
1. **Sanctioned, not sneaky** — "You're allowed to be here." Removes the fear of doing something wrong or unapproved.
2. **Built for your role, not a generic one** — accounting, sales, finance tracks — never "AI 101" with no relevance to her actual work.
3. **Proof you can point to** — real certifications (via LinkedIn Learning) that turn quiet self-doubt into a credential she can name in a meeting.
4. **You're not behind, you were never shown the door** — reframes the shame narrative: the gap was structural, not personal.
5. **A translator, not a replacement** — "We already pay for LinkedIn Learning" stops being an objection: OnRamp doesn't duplicate that investment, it makes it usable by telling each employee exactly which of the dozens of available courses actually apply to them, and by rolling already-earned certificates into one role-specific competency picture.

### Naming Direction
Avoid tech/jargon names (Quantum, Neural, Synth, .ai suffixes) — they reinforce the exact intimidation Denise feels. Lean into **doorway / on-ramp / guided-path** metaphors, echoing her own diary language ("a door I walked through instead of standing outside it").

Candidate directions:
- **Threshold** — the moment you step through, not the tech behind it
- **OnRamp** — literal, approachable, implies gradual and safe
- **Groundwork** — implies foundation-building, credibility, no shame in starting
- **Fluent** — implies capability/skill, not novice status
- **Waypoint** — implies guided progress, checkpoints, credentialing

### Tagline Options
- "AI, for the rest of us."
- "Your sanctioned path to AI confidence."
- "Built for your job — not your job title."
- "Skip the imposter syndrome. Start here."

### Visual Identity Direction
- **Palette:** warm, human, low-saturation — avoid the cold blues/purples/neon-gradient "AI tech" cliché. Consider warm neutrals + a single confident accent color (e.g., terracotta, deep teal, or amber) to signal "human-centered," not "sci-fi."
- **Typography:** approachable, readable serif or humanist sans — avoid futuristic/geometric tech fonts.
- **Imagery:** real people at real desks in real job contexts (accounting, sales floor, ops) — never abstract circuit-board or robot imagery. Denise should see herself, not a Silicon Valley stock photo.
- **Iconography:** doorways, paths, checkmarks, badges — reinforces the "sanctioned door" and "proof/credential" emotional core.

## 4. Product Requirements Document (PRD)

**Product Name (working):** OnRamp *(alt. Threshold)*
**Owner:** Meg Seidorf
**Status:** Draft — planning stage, not yet in build

### 4.1 Executive Summary
Mid-to-large companies are telling non-technical employees to "use AI more" without giving them a sanctioned, role-relevant way to learn how. This creates quiet anxiety and disengagement rather than adoption. **OnRamp** is a role-based AI adoption platform that routes employees into curated, job-specific AI training (via existing LMS infrastructure like LinkedIn Learning), maps what they've already completed against what their role actually requires so nothing is duplicated, gives them a structured, low-risk way to practice, and issues real certifications they can point to — turning a vague corporate mandate into a credible, personal skill.

**Problem it solves:** Employees like Denise (Senior Financial Analyst) know AI matters to their job security and growth, but have no sanctioned starting point, no role-specific content, no visibility into which of the dozens of courses they already have access to actually apply to them, and no way to prove they've built the skill.

**Core bet:** Adoption fails not from lack of interest, but from lack of *permission + relevance + proof*. Solve those three — and do it by mapping onto tools the company already owns rather than asking them to adopt a new one — and utilization of AI tools and training the company already pays for goes up.

### 4.2 Goals & Success Metrics
| Goal | Metric | Target (pilot) |
|---|---|---|
| Increase AI tool utilization | % active users of existing AI licenses (Copilot/ChatGPT Enterprise, etc.) | +15–20% in pilot dept within 90 days |
| Drive training engagement | Enrollment → completion rate | >60% completion (vs. typical <20% for generic LMS content) |
| Build verifiable skill | Certifications issued per employee | 1+ cert per active user in 90 days |
| Reduce time-to-value on tasks | Self-reported time saved per week | 2–3 hrs/week (pilot survey) |
| Prove leadership narrative | Manager-rated team AI proficiency | Baseline → +1 point (5-pt scale) at 90 days |
| Reduce duplicate training spend | % of employees redirected away from a course they'd already completed | Track from pilot day one (no baseline yet) |

**Non-goal:** This is not a general AI literacy course or a technical/developer-focused training product. It is also not a replacement LMS — it does not aim to host original course content at scale.

### 4.3 Target User / Primary Persona
See [Section 1: Problem Aware Avatar](#1-problem-aware-avatar) — Denise Carter, Senior Financial Analyst.

Key requirements her pain points *directly imply*:
- **"Is this even approved?"** → the product must be visibly IT/leadership-sanctioned, not a gray-area tool.
- **"Show me how it applies to what I actually do"** → content must be role/function-specific (accounting, finance, sales, ops), not generic AI 101.
- **"I don't have time to go down a rabbit hole"** → low time-investment, structured, bite-sized modules — including not re-doing training she's already completed elsewhere.
- **"No recognition at the end"** → must issue a real, shareable credential (LinkedIn Learning-backed certification).
- **Fear of looking dumb asking** → guided, judgment-free UX; no public leaderboard/exposure by default.
- **"We already pay for LinkedIn Learning, why do we need this?"** (a likely objection from budget-holders, not Denise herself) → the product must visibly integrate with, not duplicate, existing LMS spend.

### 4.4 Key Features (MVP scope)
| Feature | Description | Priority |
|---|---|---|
| Role-based learning tracks | Curated AI use-case content mapped to function (accounting, finance, sales, ops, etc.), pulling from LinkedIn Learning catalog | P0 |
| LinkedIn Learning integration | SSO + API sync to enroll, track progress, and pull completions into the platform | P0 |
| **Gap-mapping engine** | For an employee's role, shows: skill needed → what they already have access to (or have completed) that covers it → what's actually missing. Turns "40 available courses" into "these 2-3 apply to you." | **P0** |
| Certification issuance | Auto-generated certificate/badge on track completion, shareable to LinkedIn profile | P0 |
| **Certificate aggregation** | Pulls in certificates/completions already earned across connected tools (LinkedIn Learning, company LMS) so nothing is duplicated, and rolls them up into a single role-specific AI competency badge | P1 |
| Guided first-use walkthrough | Step-by-step, low-stakes intro flow (mirrors Denise's "During First Use" relief moment) | P0 |
| Manager/leadership dashboard | Rollup view of team enrollment, completion, and certification stats across all connected training sources (single dashboard instead of one per tool) | P1 |
| Champions/nudge program | Lightweight peer-champion identification + periodic role-relevant prompts | P1 |
| Usage-linked ROI reporting | Ties platform activity to AI license utilization metrics (for exec reporting) | P2 |

**Explicitly out of scope for MVP:** custom AI model access/hosting, technical/developer training tracks, gamified public leaderboards (conflicts with Denise's fear of exposure), original long-form course content (relies on existing LMS catalogs rather than competing with them).

### 4.5 Integrations & Constraints
- **LinkedIn Learning** — primary content and credentialing backbone; requires API/SSO access from IT to read enrollment, completion, and certificate history (needed for both role-based tracks and the gap-mapping engine).
- **Existing AI tool stack** (e.g., Copilot, ChatGPT Enterprise) — platform should reference/point to whichever tools are already IT-approved, not introduce new unsanctioned tools.
- **IT/Security sign-off** — critical dependency; the entire value prop hinges on the platform being visibly "approved," so this isn't optional infrastructure, it's core to the product's trust story.
- **HRIS/org data** — needed to auto-map employees to role-based tracks by department/job function.
- **Demo/pilot scope note:** a live, real-time LinkedIn Learning API integration is more than a class demo (or arguably an early pilot) needs. For demo purposes, the connected-accounts and gap-mapping screens can be mocked with representative sample data, presented transparently as "here's what it looks like once connected" rather than a working OAuth flow. Real integration work is scoped for the MVP build, not the pitch.

### 4.6 Business Case Summary
- **Primary ROI lever:** increased utilization of already-purchased AI license seats and already-purchased LMS content — reframes this as recovering value on existing spend, not new budget ask.
- **Secondary levers:** self-reported time savings, pilot-team cycle-time improvement, reduced shadow-AI risk, reduced redundant training spend (employees no longer re-taking courses they've already completed elsewhere).
- **Anticipated budget objection — "we already pay for LinkedIn Learning"** — addressed directly by positioning OnRamp as the role-specific translator and single dashboard on top of that spend, not a competing subscription.
- **Recommended validation approach:** single-department pilot (e.g., Denise's function) with leading metrics tracked from day one before company-wide rollout ask.

### 4.6a Data-to-Story: Market Sizing (for the 3-minute pitch segment)
Built using the "How Many / The Cost / The Opportunity" framework from the course pitch deck (*The Art of the SaaS Pitch*, Slide 7). Figures marked **[sourced]** come from public data; figures marked **[modeled]** are reasonable planning assumptions built on top of sourced inputs — flagged as such rather than presented as hard measurements, since that distinction matters if an evaluator pushes on the numbers.

**HOW MANY?**
- TAM: ~20,868 U.S. companies with 500+ employees **[sourced — 2019 U.S. Census Bureau data, most recent specific count found; a 2025 Economic Census refresh exists but a comparable updated total wasn't available]**. This bucket includes Denise's company size (~4,200 employees).
- Realistic early capture: ~1% → **~210 companies** **[modeled — standard conservative early-stage B2B SaaS planning assumption, not industry-specific data]**.

**THE COST?**
- 67% of employees already use AI tools at work, but only 33% of organizations train all employees on AI, and only 18% have formal AI usage/security policies **[sourced — 2026 shadow-AI industry reporting]**.
- 60% of businesses report employees have not completed comprehensive AI training, even as AI investment is set to rise 40% over the next two years **[sourced — SAP, UK data; directionally consistent with U.S. shadow-AI findings but not a U.S.-specific figure, worth flagging if asked]**.
- Translating that into dollars: enterprise L&D seat pricing (e.g., LinkedIn Learning Teams) runs **$379.88/user/year [sourced]**. A 4,200-employee company paying for seats company-wide spends roughly **$1.6M/year**; if ~60% of that isn't converting into applied skill (per the stat above), that's **~$960K/year in underutilized training spend at a single mid-size company [modeled, built on sourced inputs]**. Scaled across the ~21,000-company TAM, this points to a multi-billion-dollar underutilization problem — an order-of-magnitude story, not a precise aggregate.

**OPPORTUNITY?**
- Enterprise LMS/L&D per-seat pricing sits in the **$300–$500/user/year band [sourced — Vendr aggregated transaction data]**. OnRamp prices below that band — e.g., **$96/user/year (~$8/employee/month) [modeled]** — since it's positioned as a translator layer on top of existing LMS spend, not a full LMS replacement.
- At ~210 target companies averaging ~2,000 enrolled employees each (pilot-department rollout, not full-headcount day one — consistent with the phasing in 4.9): **420,000 seats × $96/year ≈ $40M in annual revenue potential at full capture [modeled build-up, not a Year 1 number]**.

**One-line version for the demo:** *"67% of employees are already using AI at work, but only a third of companies train them on it — that gap costs a single mid-size company like Denise's roughly $960K a year in training spend that never turns into real skill. Multiply that across the ~21,000 U.S. companies this size, and it's a multi-billion-dollar problem. OnRamp doesn't ask them to spend more — it makes the spend they've already made actually work."*

### 4.7 Brand/Positioning Summary
See [Section 3: Brand Identity](#3-brand-identity) for full detail. Positioned as a **mentor/guide**, not a tech product — warm, credible, permission-giving tone. Core promise: *"You're not behind — you were never shown the door."* Core competitive frame: *"We're not replacing what you already pay for — we're the reason it finally gets used."*

### 4.8 Risks & Open Questions
- **Risk:** If leadership frames this as mandatory/punitive rather than supportive, it recreates the exact anxiety the product is meant to solve.
- **Risk:** LinkedIn Learning content gaps for niche functions (e.g., specialized finance/loan-processing workflows) may require supplemental custom content.
- **Risk:** If the gap-mapping engine is demoed as if it's a live integration when it's actually mocked sample data, that's a credibility risk with instructors/evaluators — the demo should be explicit that this is a planned integration, not a built one.
- **Open question:** Who owns ongoing content curation — L&D, a central AI enablement team, or department champions?
- **Open question:** Should certification carry any formal weight in performance reviews, or stay reputational/self-directed?
- **Open question:** Build sequencing — does the gap-mapping engine ship in the MVP pilot, or is it a Phase 2 addition once role-based tracks and certification issuance are validated?

### 4.9 Suggested Phasing
1. **Pilot (Weeks 1–8):** Single department, manual content curation, core LinkedIn Learning integration, basic completion tracking. Gap-mapping screen may launch with manually curated (not live-API) gap data if engineering scope requires it.
2. **Validate (Weeks 9–12):** Measure against success metrics, gather qualitative feedback (Denise-style pain points resolved or not), including whether the gap-mapping view actually reduced her "which course applies to me" anxiety.
3. **Scale decision:** Present pilot results + ROI case to leadership for company-wide rollout, including the "translator, not replacement" framing for budget conversations.
